Week 4 tutorial: Generalised linear mixed models

Week 4 — Generalised linear mixed models

NoteHow to use this page

Each exercise below is a live R session. Type your answer and press Run; you can experiment freely, nothing is saved and nothing can break. The first block takes a few seconds to start while R loads in your browser.

Worked solutions are on Canvas.

Overview

So far the response has been continuous and roughly normal. This week it is a count, so the linear mixed model no longer applies directly. You extend it in the same way an ordinary linear model is extended to a GLM: a link function and a non-normal distribution, with the random effects still entering on the linear predictor scale.

That change has a cost. The likelihood of a generalised linear mixed model involves an integral over the random effects with no closed form, so it has to be approximated — and the approximations do not all agree. Much of this sheet is about seeing that for yourself.

By the end of this sheet you should be able to:

  • fit a GLM to repeated counts and recognise why it is not enough;
  • fit a Poisson GLMM with glmmPQL and with glmer;
  • explain why the GLMM likelihood cannot be evaluated exactly, and what PQL, Laplace and adaptive quadrature each do about it; and
  • say when PQL is adequate and when it is not.

The data

epil records two-week seizure counts for 59 people with epilepsy. Seizures were counted over an eight-week baseline period; patients were then randomised to progabide or placebo, and counted over four successive two-week periods. It comes with the MASS package rather than with the course package.

There are 236 rows: 59 patients by 4 periods.

Column Description
y seizure count in the two-week period — the response
trt treatment, placebo or progabide
base seizure count over the eight-week baseline
age age in years
subject patient identifier
period which of the four periods, 1 to 4
lbase centred log baseline count
lage centred log age
V4 indicator for the fourth period

lbase and lage are the centred log versions of base and age; they are what the models below use, because centring makes the intercept interpretable.

Load it with:

help(epil) gives the full description.

  1. For each placebo and treatment group, extract the epilepsy seizure counts at each period as a single vector and form a data frame with row vector containing the counts at different periods.

What this shows

The reshaped counts give one row per patient and one column per two-week period, separately for the placebo and treatment arms. This is the wide layout you need for the covariance and correlation matrices in the next item — the modelling later uses the long form.

Look at the counts themselves: they are small, skewed, and a few patients have very large values. That shape is what makes a Poisson model appropriate and a Gaussian one not.

What this shows

The correlation matrices are the point. Within a patient, seizure counts across the four periods are strongly and fairly uniformly correlated — a patient who seizes often in one period does so in all of them.

That is not a nuisance to be removed; it is real biological variation in susceptibility between patients. Ignoring it, as the GLM in item 5 does, treats each of the four periods as four independent pieces of evidence when they are much closer to one.

  1. Consider a Poisson GLM applied to the epil dataset to analyse the effect of treatment on seizure counts. Assume the seizure count follows a Poisson distribution with mean \(\lambda\), where

\[ \log(\lambda) = \eta \] \[ \eta = \beta_0 + \beta_1 (\text{lbase}) + \beta_2 (\text{trt}) + \beta_3 (\text{lbase} \times \text{trt}) + \beta_4 (\text{lage}) + \beta_5 (\text{period}). \]

Which of these covariates are statistically significant? Interpret your results.

A Poisson GLM is `glm(..., family = poisson)`. The link is log by default, so
each coefficient is a log rate ratio: `exp(coef)` is the multiplicative effect on
the expected seizure count.
The interaction `lbase * trt` asks whether the treatment effect depends on the
baseline rate.
epil.glm <- glm(y ~ lbase*trt+lage+period, family=poisson, data=epil) summary(epil.glm) 1-pchisq(869.37, 230) 
epil.glm <- glm(y ~ lbase*trt+lage+period, family=poisson, data=epil)

summary(epil.glm)

1-pchisq(869.37, 230)
What this shows

Read the coefficients as log rate ratios: exp(coef) is the multiplicative effect on the expected count. The coefficient on lbase is close to 1, meaning the seizure rate is roughly proportional to the baseline rate, which is what you would hope.

Now look at the residual deviance against its degrees of freedom, and at the chi-squared p-value computed underneath. The deviance is far larger than the degrees of freedom, so the model fits badly: there is much more variation than a Poisson with these covariates allows.

There are two possible causes and this test cannot separate them — genuine overdispersion, or the correlation within patients that the model is ignoring. Item 7 addresses the second.

  1. In Question (4) above, if the variable Period is treated as a factor with four levels, use the model to refit the data. Observe if each time period has a significant effect and interpret your results.
Wrap `period` in `factor()` so each of the four periods gets its own coefficient
instead of assuming a straight-line trend in period number.
fperiod <- as.factor(epil$period) fepil <- data.frame(epil,fperiod) fepil.glm <- glm(y~lbase*trt+lage+fperiod, family=poisson, data=fepil) summary(fepil.glm) 1-pchisq( 867.6,228)

What this shows

Treating period as a factor lets each of the four periods take its own effect instead of forcing a straight line in period number. It costs two extra parameters.

The deviance barely improves, which says the linear-in-period assumption was not the problem. That is useful negative information: the misfit is not about the shape of the time trend.

  1. Reconsider Questions (4) and (5), but this time use GLMM (PQL approach) to analyse the data, incorporating a random intercept.
`MASS` provides a penalised quasi-likelihood fitter for GLMMs. Give it a
random intercept per patient. This adds a
patient-specific intercept, so patients who seize more often than the model
expects are accounted for rather than inflating the apparent precision.
Compare the standard errors with the GLM: what happened, and why?
epil.glmm <- glmmPQL(y~lbase*trt+lage+period,random=~1|subject, family=poisson, data=epil) summary(epil.glmm) fepil.glmm <- glmmPQL(y~lbase*trt+lage+fperiod,random=~1|subject, family=poisson, data=fepil) summary(fepil.glmm)

summary(epil.glmm)

fepil.glmm <- glmmPQL(y~lbase*trt+lage+fperiod,random=~1|subject, family=poisson, data=fepil)

summary(fepil.glmm)
What this shows

The random intercept is the substantive change. Each patient now has their own baseline rate, so patients who simply seize more than their covariates predict are accounted for rather than inflating the apparent evidence.

Two things to compare with the GLM in item 5.

The standard errors are larger, particularly for the between-patient covariates such as trt. Ignoring the clustering had overstated how much independent information the data carried.

The random intercept standard deviation is substantial. That is the between-patient variation in susceptibility, and it is exactly what the Poisson GLM had to absorb as overdispersion.

One more point about interpretation. A GLMM coefficient is subject-specific: it compares the same patient under two covariate values. The GEE you meet next week estimates a population-averaged effect instead, and for a non-linear link the two are genuinely different quantities.

Q2: a binary response, and how the likelihood is approximated

The epilepsy response is a count, and for counts the approximations below behave well. Binary responses are the hard case, and they are what you meet next week, so it is worth seeing the difference here.

The ichs data in the course package record whether each of 250 preschool children had a respiratory infection at each of six visits, together with their vitamin A status. Six binary observations per child is very little information per cluster — exactly where penalised quasi-likelihood is known to struggle.

  1. Fit a logistic GLMM for respiratory infection on time, age, gender and vitamin A status, with a random intercept per child, using penalised quasi-likelihood. Report the estimated effect of vitamin A deficiency and the between-child standard deviation.
The same fitter as item 6: `glmmPQL()` from `MASS`. A random intercept per child
is `random = ~ 1 | id`. The response is 0/1, so `family = binomial`.
Read two things off the output: the `vitAdeficient` row of the coefficient
table, and the random-intercept standard deviation above it.
library(MASS) library(nlme) data(ichs, package = "lsda") ichs$vitA <- factor(ichs$vitA, levels = c(0, 1), labels = c("sufficient", "deficient")) ichs.pql <- glmmPQL(infection ~ time + age + gender + vitA, random = ~ 1 | id, family = binomial, data = ichs) summary(ichs.pql) 
library(MASS)
library(nlme)

data(ichs, package = "lsda")

ichs$vitA <- factor(ichs$vitA, levels = c(0, 1),
                    labels = c("sufficient", "deficient"))

ichs.pql <- glmmPQL(infection ~ time + age + gender + vitA,
                   random = ~ 1 | id,
                   family = binomial, data = ichs)

summary(ichs.pql)
What this shows

PQL puts the vitamin A coefficient at about 0.48 on the log-odds scale, with a between-child standard deviation of about 2.33.

That standard deviation is large. On the logit scale a value of 2.33 means children differ enormously in their underlying susceptibility — far more than the covariates explain. Keep both numbers; item 8 refits the same model by a better approximation and you will compare.

Note the interpretation. Because the random intercept is held fixed, \(\exp(0.48) \approx 1.6\) is the odds ratio for a given child — the subject-specific effect. Next week’s GEE answers a different question about the same data.

  1. Refit the same model by Laplace approximation and then by adaptive Gaussian quadrature with 25 points, and compare all three sets of estimates. Which differences matter, and what do they tell you about PQL?
`lme4::glmer()` fits a GLMM by Laplace approximation, which is `nAGQ = 1`.
Adaptive Gaussian quadrature with more points is a strictly better
approximation to the same likelihood — ask for 25 of them.
The random effect is written `(1 | id)` in `lme4`'s formula syntax.
Compare the vitamin A row across the three columns, and then the two
log-likelihoods.
library(lme4) ichs.lap <- glmer(infection ~ time + age + gender + vitA + (1 | id), family = binomial, data = ichs) ichs.agq <- update(ichs.lap, nAGQ = 25) round(cbind(PQL = summary(ichs.pql)$tTable[, "Value"], Laplace = fixef(ichs.lap), AGQ25 = fixef(ichs.agq)), 3) c(Laplace = as.numeric(logLik(ichs.lap)), AGQ25 = as.numeric(logLik(ichs.agq))) 
library(lme4)

ichs.lap <- glmer(infection ~ time + age + gender + vitA + (1 | id),
                   family = binomial, data = ichs)

round(cbind(PQL     = summary(ichs.pql)$tTable[, "Value"],
            Laplace = fixef(ichs.lap),
            AGQ25   = fixef(ichs.agq)), 3)

What this shows

The vitamin A coefficient goes 0.477 (PQL), 0.633 (Laplace), 0.604 (AGQ), and the between-child standard deviation goes 2.33, 2.72, 2.84.

PQL is the odd one out, and it is smaller on both. This is the attenuation the lectures warned about: PQL replaces the binary response by a working linear model, and with only six 0/1 observations per child that approximation is poor. It shrinks the fixed effect by about a fifth and understates the between-child variation. For the Poisson counts in Q1 the same method would have been fine — it is the combination binary response, small cluster that breaks it.

Laplace and AGQ are approximating the same likelihood, so compare their log-likelihoods: -672.3 against -666.4. Six log-likelihood units is not a rounding difference. Laplace is a one-point quadrature rule, and one point is not enough here; if the two had agreed, one point would have been enough and there would be nothing to gain from the extra work.

The practical rule: use quadrature for binary responses when you can, check that adding points stops changing the answer, and treat PQL estimates of variance components as a lower bound.

Carry the AGQ number forward. Next week you will fit the same data by GEE and get about 0.28 for vitamin A — less than half. Neither is wrong; they estimate different things, and week 5 is about which one answers your question.

Week 4 complete

What you have learnt

You have moved from Gaussian responses to counts, and from GLMs to GLMMs.

  • Why a GLM is not enough. Fitting a Poisson GLM to repeated seizure counts treats all 236 observations as independent when they come from 59 patients. The estimates may be reasonable; the standard errors are not.
  • The exponential family and the log link. For a Poisson model each coefficient is a log rate ratio, so exp(coef) is a multiplicative effect on the expected count.
  • Factor versus numeric time. Treating period as a factor lets each period have its own effect instead of assuming a straight line in period number — a weaker and often more honest assumption.
  • GLMMs by penalised quasi-likelihood. Adding a patient-specific random intercept accounts for patients who simply seize more than the covariates predict. Notice what happened to the standard errors.
  • Subject-specific interpretation. Unlike the GEE you meet next week, a GLMM coefficient describes a change for a given patient, not for the population average. For a non-linear link those are genuinely different quantities.

Check your understanding

Three questions on what you have just done. Not recorded, and you can retry as often as you like.

1. You fitted the same mean model as a Poisson GLM and then as a GLMM with a random intercept. What typically happens to the standard errors?

Ignoring the clustering overstates how much independent information the data contain, which deflates standard errors and inflates significance. This is the same failure as in week 1, now with counts. Note the coefficients themselves also shift, because a GLMM coefficient is subject-specific while the GLM's is closer to a marginal effect.

2. Treating `period` as a factor rather than as a number changed the fitted values. What assumption did that relax?

Numeric `period` forces the change from period 1 to 2 to equal the change from 3 to 4. A factor lets each period take its own value, which is a weaker and usually more honest assumption when there are only four of them. The cost is three extra parameters instead of one.

3. In the `glmmPQL` output, how would you interpret a coefficient of 0.85 on `lbase`?

With a log link, coefficients act multiplicatively, so exponentiating gives a rate ratio. Because the random intercept is held fixed it is subject-specific — it compares the same patient under two values. Reading it as a percentage change in odds would apply to a logit link, not a log link.
