  1. Express the above model in matrix form and explain the corresponding matrices/vectors.

The scalar model is

\[Y_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Day}_j + \beta_2 \text{Treatment}_i + \beta_3\,\text{Day}_j\!\times\!\text{Treatment}_i + b_{i1} + \text{Day}_j\, b_{i2} + \varepsilon_{ij}.\]

Collecting the \(11\) observations on animal \(i\) gives

\[\mathbf{Y}_i = \mathbf{X}_i\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{Z}_i\mathbf{b}_i + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_i, \qquad i = 1,\dots,60,\]

with the following pieces.

Response. \(\mathbf{Y}_i = (Y_{i1},\dots,Y_{i,11})^{\mathrm{T}}\), the \(11 \times 1\) vector of bodyweights for animal \(i\).

Fixed effects. \(\boldsymbol{\beta} = (\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3)^{\mathrm{T}}\) and \(\mathbf{X}_i\) is \(11 \times 4\). Writing \(t_j\) for the \(j\)th measurement day and \(T_i \in \{0,1\}\) for the treatment indicator of animal \(i\),

\[\mathbf{X}_i = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t_1 & T_i & t_1 T_i \\ 1 & t_2 & T_i & t_2 T_i \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & t_{11} & T_i & t_{11} T_i \end{pmatrix}.\]

Because \(T_i\) does not change within an animal, its column is constant down \(\mathbf{X}_i\). The interpretation is: \(\beta_0\) the intercept for the reference group, \(\beta_1\) its growth rate per day, \(\beta_2\) the difference in intercept between groups, and \(\beta_3\) the difference in growth rate — the parameter the experiment exists to estimate.

Random effects. \(\mathbf{b}_i = (b_{i1}, b_{i2})^{\mathrm{T}}\) is \(2 \times 1\) and

\[\mathbf{Z}_i = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t_1 \\ 1 & t_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & t_{11} \end{pmatrix} \quad (11 \times 2),\]

so each animal has its own intercept and its own slope in day, drawn from

\[\mathbf{b}_i \sim N_2(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{G}), \qquad \mathbf{G} = \begin{pmatrix} g_{11} & g_{12} \\ g_{12} & g_{22} \end{pmatrix} \ \text{positive definite}.\]

\(\mathbf{G}\) is the between-animal covariance: \(g_{11}\) how much animals differ in starting weight, \(g_{22}\) how much they differ in growth rate, \(g_{12}\) whether the two are related.

Errors. \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_i \sim N_{11}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{R}_i)\), independent of \(\mathbf{b}_i\), with \(\mathbf{R}_i\) an \(11\times 11\) positive definite matrix describing within-animal variation not explained by the random effects — measurement error plus any serial correlation.

Marginal distribution. Integrating out \(\mathbf{b}_i\),

\[\mathbf{Y}_i \sim N_{11}\!\left(\mathbf{X}_i\boldsymbol{\beta},\; \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i\right), \qquad \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i = \mathbf{Z}_i\mathbf{G}\mathbf{Z}_i^{\mathrm{T}} + \mathbf{R}_i.\]

Two remarks worth making explicitly:

  1. Even if \(\mathbf{R}_i = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}\), the responses on one animal are still correlated, because \(\mathbf{Z}_i\mathbf{G}\mathbf{Z}_i^{\mathrm{T}}\) is not diagonal. The random effects alone induce within-animal correlation.
  2. With a random slope, that induced correlation is not stationary: the variance \(\mathbf{z}_j^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{z}_j + R_{i,jj}\) grows with \(t_j\), since \(\mathbf{z}_j = (1, t_j)^{\mathrm{T}}\). Animals fan out over time. This is why the random-slope model often fits growth data better than a random intercept alone.

The design here is balanced — all 60 animals are measured on the same 11 days — so \(\mathbf{X}_i\), \(\mathbf{Z}_i\) and \(\mathbf{R}_i\) have the same dimensions for every \(i\), and \(\mathbf{Z}_i\) is in fact the same matrix for all animals.

  1. In R, use the function `lme( )’ to analyze the data by specifying \(\mathbf{G}\) as general covariance structure and \(\mathbf{R}_i\) as
    2.1. independent covariance structure,
The independent structure is the default: give `lme()` no `correlation`
argument at all. Keep `random = ~ day | subject` so G stays general.
# Stage 1 compares R_i structures with a random intercept only. A random slope # here stops ARMA(1,1) converging -- see the explanation below. lme.ide <- lme(Y ~ day * treatment, random = ~ 1 | subject, data = cg, method = "ML") round(fixef(lme.ide), 3) 
# Stage 1 compares R_i structures with a random intercept only. A random slope
# here stops ARMA(1,1) converging -- see the explanation below.
lme.ide <- lme(Y ~ day * treatment, random = ~ 1 | subject,
               data = cg, method = "ML")
round(fixef(lme.ide), 3)
What this shows

The four fixed effects are the intercept, the growth rate, the group difference in level, and the group difference in slope — the interaction, which is the parameter the experiment exists to estimate.

Note the random intercept only at this stage. The question specifies a random slope as well, and you will add it in item 9. Comparing R_i structures with the simpler G first is deliberate: with a random slope, ARMA(1,1) does not converge on these data, because a random slope already makes the marginal correlation depend on the gap between days and an ARMA error does the same thing. The two compete to explain one feature, and the likelihood has no clear maximum.

2.2. compound symmetry covariance structure,\
`update()` saves retyping the whole call — pass only what changes.
Compound symmetry means every pair of times is equally correlated, however far
apart. The `nlme` constructor for it is listed in `?corClasses`; its `form`
argument names the grouping, here `~ 1 | subject`.
lme.cs <- update(lme.ide, correlation = corCompSymm(form = ~ 1 | subject)) round(coef(lme.cs$modelStruct$corStruct, unconstrained = FALSE), 3) 
lme.cs <- update(lme.ide, correlation = corCompSymm(form = ~ 1 | subject))
round(coef(lme.cs$modelStruct$corStruct, unconstrained = FALSE), 3)
What this shows

Compound symmetry estimates a single correlation shared by every pair of days, however far apart. Compare it with the AR(1) estimate in the next item.

2.3. AR(1) structure,\
Now correlation should decay with the gap in days rather than being constant —
an autoregressive structure of order 1. Its `form` needs the time variable as
well as the grouping, so `~ day | subject`.
lme.ar1 <- update(lme.ide, correlation = corAR1(form = ~ day | subject)) round(coef(lme.ar1$modelStruct$corStruct, unconstrained = FALSE), 3) 
lme.ar1 <- update(lme.ide, correlation = corAR1(form = ~ day | subject))
round(coef(lme.ar1$modelStruct$corStruct, unconstrained = FALSE), 3)
What this shows

The AR(1) parameter. Correlation between residuals now decays as \(\rho^{|s-t|}\), so adjacent days are strongly related and distant days barely so — a much more plausible story for growth measurements than one constant correlation.

2.4. ARMA(1,1) structure,

where the option `method’ in R is the maximum likelihood (ML) estimation method. Select the best structure of \(\mathbf{R}_i\) in terms of the resulting AIC or BIC value.

The ARMA constructor needs both orders given explicitly, `p` and `q`.
There is a single function that takes several fitted `lme` objects and returns
a comparison table with AIC, BIC and likelihood ratio tests. They are nested, so the
likelihood ratio tests in that table are meaningful.
lme.arma <- update(lme.ide, correlation = corARMA(form = ~ day | subject, p = 1, q = 1)) anova(lme.ide, lme.cs, lme.ar1, lme.arma) 
lme.arma <- update(lme.ide, correlation = corARMA(form = ~ day | subject, p = 1, q = 1))
anova(lme.ide, lme.cs, lme.ar1, lme.arma)
What this shows

anova() on nested fits gives AIC, BIC and likelihood ratio tests together.

AR(1) and ARMA(1,1) both improve enormously on independence and compound symmetry — around 440 units of AIC. Between the two the difference is small: ARMA edges it on both criteria, but by about 7 units of AIC and only 2 of BIC, which is close to a tie.

Here you meet a practical constraint that model selection tables do not show. ARMA(1,1) cannot be fitted alongside the richer G structures you are about to compare, so continuing with it would mean abandoning the second stage entirely. AR(1) is chosen for that reason, and because BIC separates the two by almost nothing. That is a defensible decision, but it is a decision, and it belongs in the write-up rather than being made silently.

  1. With the selected structure for \(\mathbf{R}_i\), select the best structure for \(\mathbf{G}\) among the candidates below:
    3.1. independent covariance structure,
Now hold `correlation` fixed and vary `random`. The most restrictive option forces the random intercept and slope to have equal
variance and zero covariance. Look for the `pd` constructor meaning "identity".
# Stage 2: hold AR(1) and vary G. The general structure needs a different # optimiser, and ARMA(1,1) will not fit these G structures at all. ctrl <- lmeControl(opt = "optim") lme.G.ident <- update(lme.ar1, random = pdIdent(~ day), control = ctrl) round(as.numeric(VarCorr(lme.G.ident)[, "StdDev"]), 4) 
# Stage 2: hold AR(1) and vary G. The general structure needs a different
# optimiser, and ARMA(1,1) will not fit these G structures at all.
ctrl <- lmeControl(opt = "optim")
lme.G.ident <- update(lme.ar1, random = pdIdent(~ day), control = ctrl)
round(as.numeric(VarCorr(lme.G.ident)[, "StdDev"]), 4)
What this shows

Now the random slope appears. pdIdent is the most restrictive G: equal variances for intercept and slope, and no covariance between them.

control = lmeControl(opt = "optim") switches optimiser. With the default the general structure does not converge here. If a mixed model fails to converge, trying another optimiser is a reasonable first step — but if several fail, treat that as evidence the model is asking too much of the data rather than a technical nuisance.

3.2. diagonal covariance structure,\
The next-least-restrictive option allows different variances but still no
covariance between the random intercept and slope — a diagonal G.
lme.G.diag <- update(lme.ar1, random = pdDiag(~ day), control = ctrl) round(as.numeric(VarCorr(lme.G.diag)[, "StdDev"]), 4) 
lme.G.diag <- update(lme.ar1, random = pdDiag(~ day), control = ctrl)
round(as.numeric(VarCorr(lme.G.diag)[, "StdDev"]), 4)
What this shows

pdDiag allows the intercept and slope to have different variances but still forces their covariance to zero. The two standard deviations printed are on quite different scales, because one is a weight and the other a weight per day.

3.3. general covariance structure.

Again, the option `method’ in R is the maximum likelihood (ML) estimation method.

The general structure is the unrestricted one you already fitted. Compare all
three with `anova()`.
lme.G.gen <- update(lme.ar1, random = ~ day | subject, control = ctrl) anova(lme.G.ident, lme.G.diag, lme.G.gen) 
lme.G.gen <- update(lme.ar1, random = ~ day | subject, control = ctrl)
anova(lme.G.ident, lme.G.diag, lme.G.gen)
What this shows

The three G structures are nested, so the likelihood ratio tests in this table are meaningful.

If the diagonal structure is not clearly beaten by the general one, prefer it: the extra covariance parameter is buying little, and a simpler G is easier to interpret and more stable to fit.

  1. Refit the selected model so far, but using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation method this time.
Refit with `method = "REML"`. ML underestimates variance components because it
ignores the degrees of freedom used estimating the fixed effects; REML corrects
for that. Now that the structure is settled, REML gives better variance
estimates.
Try `run_app("lmm_simulator")` with a small number of subjects to watch this

final <- update(lme.G.diag, method = "REML") round(fixef(final), 3) 
final <- update(lme.G.diag, method = "REML")
round(fixef(final), 3)
What this shows

Refitting with REML now that the structure is settled. ML underestimates variance components because it does not pay for having estimated the fixed effects; REML corrects that. The fixed effects barely move — it is the variance components that change.

run_app("lmm_simulator") with a small number of subjects shows this directly.

Random effects are not residuals, so the formula inside `qqnorm()` must extract

qqnorm(final, ~ ranef(.)) 
qqnorm(final, ~ ranef(.))
What this shows

This is a different assumption from the last plot. Here you are checking \(\mathbf{b}_i \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{G})\) — that the animals are normally distributed around the population, not that the residuals are. Points close to the line support it; a few stragglers are worth identifying, since they are individual animals behaving unusually rather than individual measurements.

5.3. Give the 95\% confidence intervals for the estimates of fixed effects and

variance components. Explain whether or not they are significant.

One `nlme` function returns confidence intervals for the fixed effects, the
variance components and the correlation parameters together. An interval for a variance
that nearly touches zero is telling you something.
intervals(final, which = "fixed")

What this shows

Confidence intervals for the fixed effects. The interaction is the one to read: if its interval excludes zero, the two groups genuinely differ in growth rate, which is the scientific question.

Interpret the interval, not just whether it contains zero. An interval from 0.01 to 0.9 excludes zero but is consistent with an effect too small to matter.

Week 3 complete

What you have learnt

You have taken a linear mixed model from specification to a checked, interpreted fit.

  • Writing the model in matrix form, and seeing why the random slope makes the marginal variance grow with time — animals fan out.
  • Model selection in two stages: settle \(\mathbf{R}_i\) first with \(\mathbf{G}\) held general, then settle \(\mathbf{G}\). Doing both at once searches a much larger space for no good reason.
  • ML versus REML. ML is required when comparing models with different fixed effects; REML gives less biased variance components once the structure is fixed. Refitting the chosen model with REML is the standard last step.
  • Diagnostics. Standardised residuals against fitted values for constant spread; a QQ plot of residuals; and separately a QQ plot of the predicted random effects, which checks \(\mathbf{b}_i \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{G})\) and is a different assumption from the residual one.
  • Inference and prediction. Confidence intervals for fixed effects and variance components together, and subject-specific predictions that track individual animals rather than the group mean.

Worth trying: run_app("lmm_simulator") with a small number of subjects — you can watch ML underestimate the between-subject standard deviation while REML does not.

Check your understanding

Three questions on what you have just done. Not recorded, and you can retry as often as you like.

1. You selected a covariance structure using ML, then refitted the chosen model with REML. Why not simply use REML throughout?

REML works with error contrasts that depend on the fixed-effects design matrix, so changing the fixed effects changes what is being maximised and the likelihoods cannot be compared. ML is the right tool for selection and REML for the final variance estimates — which is exactly the order the tutorial used. The claim that ML gives better variance estimates has it backwards: ML variance estimates are biased downwards.

2. The QQ plot of residuals looked fine but the QQ plot of the predicted random effects showed two clear outliers. What does that suggest?

These are two separate assumptions and each needs its own check. The residual plot asks whether the model describes the data *within* an animal; the random-effects plot asks whether animals are normally distributed *around* the population mean. Two unusual animals is worth investigating — are they real, or data errors? — before deciding what to do.

3. `intervals()` gave a confidence interval for one variance component that extended almost to zero. How should you read that?

An interval nearly touching zero says the data are consistent with that random effect being unnecessary. Testing it formally runs into the boundary problem: the null sits on the edge of the parameter space, so the usual $\chi^2_1$ reference distribution is wrong and the naive test under-rejects. A near-zero variance estimate is a signal to consider the simpler model, not a failure.
